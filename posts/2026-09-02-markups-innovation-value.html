Firms with the most market power make the most valuable inventions, for everyone

2 September 2026

Whether market power helps or hinders innovation is one of the oldest open questions in the field. Schumpeter argued that monopoly rents fund risky research and let firms recoup it. Arrow argued the opposite, that a comfortable incumbent has little reason to cannibalise a profitable product. Later work reconciles them with an inverted U, and the empirical literature has produced results in every direction.

Most of that literature measures innovation with R&D spending or patent counts. Both are inputs or tallies rather than value, and neither says anything about the part of the return the innovating firm does not capture. With Ralf Martin, Jenniffer Solorzano Mosquera and Catherine Thomas, I revisit the question using measures of what innovation is actually worth, split into the private value the firm keeps and the spillover value that flows to everyone building on it.

The sample is 14,681 firms across 23 EU countries and 2,415 US firms, all granted patents between 2005 and 2014 and matched to their financial accounts. Markups are a firm-level Lerner index adjusted for returns to scale, which credits firms for genuine scale economies rather than counting them as market power. Innovation values come from pushing patent-level private values through the global citation network, so an invention’s spillover value is the share of downstream value that traces back to it.

The association is positive and large. Comparing firms within the same year, a standard deviation more markup goes with innovation worth around 29% more. That comparison is generous, so we tighten it: within country the figure is 13%, and within industry-country groups it is 12%. The standard deviation of markups here is 0.84, so these are not knife-edge differences between firms. There is no sign of the inverted U.

The part we found most interesting concerns spillovers. Private and spillover value are correlated but far from identical, which leaves room for high-markup firms to be producing innovation that is lucrative for them and useless to anyone else. They are not. A standard deviation more markup goes with spillovers worth 22% more, and 9% more within year and industry-country group.

But condition on the firm’s private innovation value and that advantage nearly disappears, falling to about 2%. So high-markup firms create more social value because they create more valuable innovation overall, not because their innovation is unusually generous to others. That is a more modest claim than the raw correlation suggests, and it is the one the data support.

We can also run the arrow the other way, since markups and concentration have both been rising and one story is that today’s market power was bought by yesterday’s intangible investment. Building a depreciated stock of each firm’s past innovation value, we find it positively correlated with current markups — and conditioning on it barely dents the main result. A standard deviation higher markup is still associated with 7% more valuable innovation and 6% more valuable spillovers once that initial characteristic is taken into account. Past innovation explains only a small share of the markup variation here.

None of this is causal, and we are careful not to claim otherwise. What it does establish is that the welfare accounting for market power is incomplete if it stops at prices. The firms charging the largest margins are also producing the innovation others build on most valuably, and a competition policy treating markups as pure deadweight loss is ignoring one side of that ledger.

The paper is Firm Markups and the Economic Value of Innovation, with Ralf Martin, Jenniffer Solorzano Mosquera and Catherine Thomas, in the International Journal of Industrial Organization.
