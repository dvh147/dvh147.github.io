Over half of the world's knowledge spillovers are invisible to citation counts

2 September 2026

The case for subsidising R&D rests on a gap between what an inventor captures and what an invention is worth to everyone else. If that gap were the same size in every technology, the right policy would be a uniform subsidy, and most countries behave as though it is. But if the gap varies a lot across fields, an untargeted subsidy is leaving money on the table.

Measuring the gap has been the obstacle. The standard proxy for how much an invention spills over is its count of forward citations. That measure has two problems. It treats every citation as equally valuable, when invention values are extraordinarily skewed. And it only sees direct citations, when much of the influence of foundational work arrives through long chains — A enables B, which enables C, and only C is commercially large.

With Charlotte Guillard, Ralf Martin, Pierre Mohnen and Catherine Thomas, I take a different route. We start from just over 15 million inventions patented worldwide between 2005 and 2014. For about 600,000 of them, Kogan and co-authors recover a private value from the abnormal stock return around the day the patent was granted. We extend those values to the rest by binning patents on class, timing, family size and claim count, then push value backwards through the global citation network, assigning each invention a share of the value of everything that builds on it directly or indirectly. The result is P-Rank, a value-weighted analogue of the algorithm that made Google’s search work.

An invention’s spillover value is its P-Rank minus its private value. Comparing that to its citation count identifies what we call Hidden Giants — inventions whose real influence is at least partly concealed by a plain count of the citations they received. They are not a curiosity. Hidden Giants account for 54% of the value of all global knowledge spillovers. University patents are heavily overrepresented among them, which is what you would expect if universities are doing the foundational work that others build on.

The second half asks what to do with this. We model inventors drawing ideas from a field-specific distribution of quality and developing only those above a private cost threshold, and fit that model to 410 technology field-year cells. A subsidy lowers the threshold, pulling in ideas that are privately marginal but socially valuable. The return on that subsidy — we call it IStraX — is high when the cost is low, when many ideas sit just under the threshold, and when the inventions it unlocks spill over generously.

Across 41 fields and 43 countries, IStraX in a country’s best field is routinely three or four times its worst. Targeting on it would have raised national returns by 40% to 80% over a non-discriminating policy. Coordinating across the OECD, so that countries stop optimising against each other, adds about 30% on top.

The obvious objection is that governments are bad at picking winners, and the record on that is not encouraging. Our answer is that the rankings are persistent enough to be useful without any forecasting: a policy that ranks fields purely on historical information captures about two-thirds of what the ex-post optimal policy would have earned. The best field also differs by country in ways a general rule would miss — wireless in the US, IT methods for management in China, clean cars in Germany.

The caveat worth stating is that all of this rests on extrapolating stock-market valuations to patents that never had one, and on a model of how ideas arrive. Neither is innocuous. What we would defend is the relative ranking rather than the absolute level.

The paper is Efficient Industrial Policy for Innovation: Standing on the Shoulders of Hidden Giants, with Charlotte Guillard, Ralf Martin, Pierre Mohnen and Catherine Thomas.
