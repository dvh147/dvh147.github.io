Patent citations cannot tell prize-winning science from ordinary science

2 September 2026

Public money for science is justified by spillovers. Researchers capture a small share of what their work is eventually worth, so a market left alone underfunds it. That argument is old and well accepted, and it is also, in practice, hard to act on, because nobody can say how much a given body of science is spilling over or where the value lands.

The usual approach counts how often patents cite scientific papers. Ralf Martin, Arjun Shah, Anna Valero and I think that measure is much weaker than its popularity suggests, and we can show it.

The problem is chains. A paper rarely turns into a product. It enables another paper, which enables a third, which eventually informs a patent that makes money. Ahmadpoor and Jones showed that many of the economically important links between science and technology are indirect. Direct patent-to-paper citations see none of that. They also treat every citation alike, when the value of patents is so skewed that a handful of inventions account for most of the money.

We build Science Rank. Papers and patents are nodes in one combined citation network. We take the private value of a patented invention and push a share of it back to everything the invention relied on, directly or indirectly. A paper’s Science Rank is what accumulates from all the downstream technology it helped make possible, weighted by what that technology was actually worth.

The test we set for it is whether it recognises science that scientists already recognise. We take work that won a Nobel, a Fields Medal or a Turing Award, together with papers singled out by the editors of Nature and Science, and ask where it falls in the distribution. 98.9% of it lands above the median of Science Rank. On direct patent-to-paper citation counts, the same prize-winning work splits roughly fifty-fifty around the median — which is to say that measure is close to uninformative about whether a paper mattered.

We should be careful about what that comparison establishes. Prizes are not a perfect proxy for downstream economic value, and some celebrated science has no commercial route at all. The claim is narrower: a measure that cannot separate Nobel-winning work from the median paper is a weak instrument for allocating research money, and the standard one cannot.

What the measure then buys you is a map. Because value flows have an origin and a destination, Science Rank can be partitioned both ways. The United States stands out twice over, generating spillovers at enormous scale and also capturing an unusually large share of them domestically. Splitting by destination technology, the share of a country’s science spillovers that flow into clean innovation is higher in poorer countries and falls as GDP per capita rises — which is not the direction most people would guess.

The honest limitation is that the absolute magnitudes depend on an assumption about how much of innovation is prior knowledge rather than private effort. Change that parameter and the levels move. We have not yet run the sensitivity analysis that would settle how much, and until we do, the rankings are more trustworthy than the numbers attached to them. This is also measurement rather than causal inference: it says where spillovers originate and land, not what one more pound of research funding would buy.

The paper is Spillovers from Science, with Ralf Martin, Arjun Shah and Anna Valero, CEP Discussion Paper 2165.
