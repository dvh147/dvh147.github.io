The clean innovation slump looks partly like a loss of nerve

2 September 2026

Clean energy patenting has been falling since around 2010, at roughly 5.5% a year. Several explanations are on the table: cheap gas, the exhaustion of easy clean ideas, weaker willingness to pay. Ashley Langer, Derek Lemoine, Ralf Martin and I look at a different one, which is that the financial crisis changed how much risk innovators were willing to carry, and clean innovation is where the risk was.

The argument runs through the shape of the value distribution rather than its average. We measure the private value of an invention the way Kogan and co-authors do, from the abnormal stock return in the three days around a patent grant. Two things about that measure needed fixing for our purposes. It only recovers value to the extent the grant was a surprise, and the standard correction assumes the market puts a fixed 0.56 probability on any application being granted — the average US grant rate between 1991 and 2001. Grant rates differ a lot by technology, so we recompute that probability field by field. The measure also ignores what the research cost, which matters if what you care about is the return rather than the payoff, so we back out development costs by fitting a model of the innovation decision to the observed value distribution.

Two descriptive facts follow. Clean inventions are on average more valuable than dirty ones, and their values are more dispersed — a fatter tail in both directions. And after the onset of the Great Recession, the dispersion of both values and returns compresses, for clean and dirty alike.

Compression is what a shift towards safer projects looks like. If clean and dirty inventions sit differently in the tails of that distribution, and they do, then a general retreat from risk is not neutral between them. It falls harder on clean.

To size that, we construct a counterfactual: hold the pre-recession distribution of values, or of returns, fixed, and ask what the clean share would have been. Even on our most conservative version, the clean share in 2014 would have been no lower than its actual 2011 peak. Instead of a clean drop, a clean plateau.

The result needs one important qualification, and it cuts against the headline. Financial constraints explain the fall in clean innovation relative to dirty. They do not explain the fall in absolute terms — in the counterfactual there is still a decline, it simply starts from a higher peak. So this is an account of why clean lost ground, not of why innovation in energy slowed down generally.

We also cannot rule out the competing stories. Changes in fuel prices or new fossil technologies may have made dirty more attractive. Falling real wages may have cut willingness to pay for clean products. The clean wave from 1990 to 2011 may simply have used up the ideas that were easy to have. Our contribution is to show that a mechanism nobody was looking at is large enough to matter, which we view as a reason to look harder at it rather than a reason to stop looking at the others.

If it holds up, the policy reading is uncomfortable. Financial conditions after a crisis are not usually treated as climate policy, and on this evidence they act like it — a credit shock quietly reallocates innovation away from the technologies the transition depends on, at exactly the moment governments are least able to spend.

The paper is The Private Value of Clean Energy Innovation, with Ashley Langer, Derek Lemoine and Ralf Martin.
