Putting an inventor in an unfamiliar field costs a firm 25% more

2 September 2026

In 1945 Raytheon filed a patent on the microwave oven. Percy Spencer, who invented it, had spent his career on radar, learning to use vacuum tubes to generate microwave signals. Pointed at cooking rather than at aircraft detection, that knowledge produced one of the century’s more consequential domestic technologies.

The pattern generalises. Inventors who have worked across several technological fields have a comparative advantage in producing the highest-value inventions, which is one of the more robust findings in the field. And yet Benjamin Jones documented that the probability of an inventor switching fields has been falling for decades. His explanation is that the frontier keeps moving outwards, so retraining into a new field costs more than it used to.

That explanation should weaken as fields get narrower, because skills transfer more easily between neighbouring specialities than between distant ones. Jordan Bisset and I check this at the level of CPC groups, which cut the technological space into 9,474 classes. The share of intellectually mobile inventors is falling there too. So something other than the distance to the frontier is at work.

We propose that the constraint is inside the firm. Think of the inventors a firm can put on a given technological field as three groups: those who have already invented in it, those who have invented but only elsewhere, and those who have never invented at all. If the firm simply maximises the ideas it gets, the mix it chooses should track how productive each group actually is in that field. If instead it is more expensive to move someone across a boundary, the firm will use fewer outsiders and newcomers than their productivity warrants, and the size of that gap tells you the size of the friction.

Building a panel of US inventors from granted patents, matched to firms and to technological fields, we estimate the productivity of each group and compare it to the mix actually observed. The data are detailed enough to absorb firm-by-year and firm-by-field fixed effects, and in some specifications firm-by-year-by-field, which soaks up most of what a firm might know about a field that we do not.

Our most conservative estimates say it costs a firm 25% more to bring in an inventor from another field, and 18% more to bring in a first-time inventor, than to use someone already working there. Those figures rise when idea production is weighted by quality. The misallocation is therefore worst precisely where it is most expensive to society: the gap between who is used and who would be most productive is largest for the ideas with the highest value.

Because we estimate frictions at the level of individual firms and fields, rather than one economy-wide number, the variation is visible — there is no reason switching within semiconductors should cost what switching within biotechnology costs, and it does not. In counterfactuals, even moderate reductions in these frictions raise idea generation by around 50%.

What we do not have is the mechanism. A friction estimated as a residual gap between productivity and allocation is consistent with several stories — managers cannot observe transferable skill, internal labour markets are sticky, teams resist outsiders — and they have different remedies. The number says the problem is worth solving. It does not say how.

The paper is Intellectual Mobility Frictions, with Jordan Bisset.
