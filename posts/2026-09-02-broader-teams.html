Broader teams invent better things. Broadening teams does not.

2 September 2026

Teams that span more technological fields produce more valuable inventions. It is one of the more robust regularities in the economics of innovation: it shows up across patent offices, across decades, and across most of the ways you might choose to measure breadth, and it survives the controls you would think to add.

The policy reading is immediate, and it is everywhere. If breadth is good, broaden the teams. Managers are told to staff across specialisms, and funders are told to reward consortia that cross disciplinary lines.

That inference does not follow, and the reason is mundane. Nobody assigns inventors to teams at random. The firms that can assemble broad teams are the firms with the slack to assemble them, and the inventors who get pulled onto those teams are disproportionately the ones already doing good work. Breadth travels with the conditions that produce good inventions, which is enough to generate the correlation without breadth doing any of the work.

The question a manager or a funder actually faces is a different one: what happens if you change breadth and hold the rest fixed. To get at that I use a blunt source of variation, the death of a team member. When an inventor dies mid-project the team that carries on is narrower than the one that started, and the narrowing is plausibly unrelated to how well the remaining work was going to go.

Using that variation, I find no evidence that team breadth raises the quality of what the team subsequently invents. The cross-sectional relationship, meanwhile, does not survive accounting for who ends up on broad teams, where positive selection does most of the work. The correlation is real — the causal reading of it is not.

I want to be careful about what this does and does not say. It is not a finding that diverse expertise is unproductive, and it is not an argument against assembling teams that cross fields. It is narrower than that, and more useful for being narrower: at current margins, in this population, pushing breadth above where firms already put it is not where the returns are. If you want more valuable inventions, the binding constraint is more likely who is in the room than how many fields they collectively cover.

That is a deflating result, and deflating results of this kind are undersupplied. The literature on team composition has produced a great many correlations and very few designs that can separate the mix from the people. Until more of those designs exist, I would treat “broaden the team” as an untested recommendation rather than a settled one.

The paper is Do Broader Teams Lead to More Innovation?, currently under review at Management Science.
