Clean innovation is a win-win, and most of the winnings leave the country

2 September 2026

There is a familiar worry about supporting clean technology, and it is a reasonable one. If clean innovation generates smaller knowledge spillovers than other fields, then directing money towards it buys climate benefit at the cost of growth. That tradeoff would have to be weighed against the environmental externality, which is large but notoriously hard to pin down, and the argument becomes unresolvable in the way that arguments about the social cost of carbon usually are.

Ralf Martin and I think the tradeoff does not exist. We estimate the return to a marginal R&D subsidy field by field, where the return is the knowledge spillover value created by the extra inventions the subsidy pulls into existence, for inventions patented between 2009 and 2018 across 42 countries.

Clean comes out at a global return rate of 135%, second of six broad fields and beaten only by electrical engineering. The weighted average across fields is 120%, so a scheme aimed at clean returns about an eighth more than one that simply spreads money in proportion to how big each field already is. Inside clean, smart systems and offshore wind do better than artificial intelligence and biotechnology. The clean advantage sits between 13% and 18% above the average in China, the EU, Japan and the US, and is smaller in Korea at 4%.

So the growth-versus-climate framing is wrong on its own terms. Clean support is not a sacrifice of growth that has to be justified by the climate benefit — it earns its keep on spillovers alone, and the climate benefit comes on top.

The more interesting finding is about who collects. Because our measure tracks value through the citation network, we can follow spillovers from origin to destination. Only about 23% of clean spillover value stays inside the country that produced it. Judged on national returns rather than global ones, clean stops looking special: its advantage falls to roughly the average across fields. In Europe the local case is actually stronger. In the US it vanishes entirely.

That gap between what is globally optimal and what is nationally optimal is the whole argument for coordination. Suppose subsidising wind in Germany returns 30 cents on the dollar inside Germany and 40 globally, while smart grids return 20 domestically and 80 globally. Germany chooses wind. The world would rather it chose grids. We simulate exactly this, and coordination is worth a great deal: an EU-wide optimal policy returns a quarter more to the EU than the sum of nationally optimal ones, an OECD scheme about 40% more, and a global scheme over 60%.

The global number comes with a political problem attached, which we should not skate over. It is driven almost entirely by directing money to Chinese clean innovation, which absorbs over 80% of subsidies under a globally optimal allocation. That is a defensible answer to the question we asked and an impossible answer to sell. Interestingly, coordination among the G7 alone gets close to the OECD result, which is a more plausible starting point.

One last application. Europe has worried that the Inflation Reduction Act’s $400 billion disadvantages European firms. Our estimates say that a million dollars of US clean subsidy generates $1.26 million of spillovers in Europe, against $1.18 million from an untargeted US subsidy. Europe therefore gains about eight cents on the dollar from the US choosing clean specifically. The reverse flow is weaker: European clean support returns $1.13 million to the US, slightly less than an untargeted European subsidy would. Whether that offsets the commercial losses is a separate question we do not answer, but it belongs in the debate.

The paper is Knowledge Spillovers from Clean Innovation. A Tradeoff between Growth and Climate?, with Ralf Martin, under revision at Nature Climate Change.
