How many citations a patent has depends on who is counting

2 September 2026

Counting the citations a patent receives is the closest thing innovation economics has to a standard measure of impact. It is used to weight patents by quality, to rank firms and countries, and to identify breakthroughs. It looks like a simple count.

It is not one number, it is a family of numbers, and which one you get depends on three choices that are usually made without comment. Which patent office the focal application sits in. Whether you count citations arriving from other offices or only from the same one. And whether you treat the several documents covering one invention in different jurisdictions as one patent family or as separate patents.

There are reasons to expect these choices to matter. Examiners cite prior art from their own jurisdiction disproportionately, so office choice imports a home bias. The American duty of candor obliges applicants to disclose prior art they know of, which is one reason US documents carry more references than European ones on average — and Sampat found that in roughly 70% of US patents the foreign references were put there by the applicant rather than the examiner. Restrict to citations from a single office and you may be measuring national relevance while believing you are measuring global impact.

With Jurriën Bakker, Lin Zhang and Bart Van Looy, I set out to find out how much this matters, on 8.7 million applications from PATSTAT — 4.4 million at the US office, 2.3 million at the European office, and 1.9 million filed through the international route, with filing dates from 1970 to 2011. For each we compute the indicator every reasonable combination of those three choices produces, then ask how similar the results are.

They are not very similar. The indicators differ substantially from one another, and the divergence is worse where it matters most. Because so much empirical work identifies breakthrough inventions as the top tail of the citation distribution, we look specifically at the overlap between the sets of patents each method flags as highly cited. Different methods disagree about which patents those are.

Correcting for patent families is what stabilises things, and the broader of the two definitions, the extended INPADOC family, gives the most uniform results across the other choices. That is the practical recommendation, and it is not costless — family correction requires linking documents across offices, which is why single-office counts remain popular.

The wider point is one about defaults. A researcher choosing the American office and its own citations is not making a neutral choice, they are choosing a particular mix of home bias and examiner practice, and the resulting sample of breakthrough patents is partly an artefact of that choice. The literature has generally treated the measurement decision as innocuous. It is not, and it deserves to be stated and defended rather than inherited from whichever database was convenient.

The paper is Patent Citation Indicators: One Size Fits All?, with Jurriën Bakker, Lin Zhang and Bart Van Looy, in Scientometrics.
