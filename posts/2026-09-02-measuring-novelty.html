Novel inventions get fewer citations on average, and that is the point

2 September 2026

Almost every empirical study of radical invention identifies its subject after the fact, by looking for inventions that turned out to matter — heavily cited patents, products that reshaped a market. That is convenient and it builds in two problems.

The first is selection on success. Inventions with the potential to be radical that did not get there are, by construction, absent. If you want to understand what makes novelty pay off, a sample containing only the payoffs cannot tell you. The second is timing and attribution. Novel approaches usually need years of refinement, and the invention that finally lands the impact is often not the one that introduced the departure. Measuring after the fact credits the wrong patent, and only once it is too late to be useful.

With Jurriën Bakker and Reinhilde Veugelers, I propose measuring novelty before any of that happens, from what a patent document says about itself. Following Brian Arthur, we treat a technology as a means of fulfilling a purpose, which gives two distinct ways a technology can be new. It can be novel in recombination — putting together components and principles in a combination not seen before. Or it can be novel in its knowledge origins, drawing on fields never previously used for this kind of problem, which we split into technological and scientific origins. Patent classifications and citations let us compute all three for every patented invention since 1980.

The measures are only as good as their validation, so we do three. We compare against the established measures of adjacent constructs, Trajtenberg’s originality and Shane’s radicalness, and find our indicators correlated with them but better behaved on the properties novelty should have. We check inventions that R&D Magazine judged among the most technologically novel of their year, and find them overrepresented among our high scorers. And we check the mirror image, patents the European Patent Office refused for lacking novelty or inventive step, and find them underrepresented — a false-positive test, which measures of this kind rarely bother to run.

Then the result that reframes the whole exercise. We ask what technological impact novel inventions go on to have, using forward citations with a family correction over a seven-year window. Novelty in recombination and in technological origins lowers the average number of citations received, and raises the dispersion. Novelty in scientific origins raises the average. Inventions scoring on all three raise both.

A measure that predicted higher average impact would be a measure of quality. What we have is a measure of risk. The typical novel invention does worse than the typical conventional one, and novel inventions are far likelier to end up in the extreme right tail — among the roughly 3% that are two standard deviation outliers in their class and year, or the 0.5% that are five. That is the shape you would expect if novelty is the raw material of radical change and most attempts fail.

The limitation is inherited from the data. Patent classifications and citations are an imperfect record of what an invention actually did, and they see nothing that was never patented. What the measure buys is the ability to study novelty and its consequences separately, instead of defining one by the other.

The paper is Measuring Technological Novelty with Patent-based Indicators, with Jurriën Bakker and Reinhilde Veugelers, in Research Policy.
