Four words for exceptional innovation, and the field cannot tell them apart

2 September 2026

The literature on exceptional innovation has four main words for its subject — radical, breakthrough, discontinuous, disruptive — and twenty years ago two well-known papers warned that they were being used interchangeably in ways that would make results hard to compare. Whether that warning worked is an empirical question, and with Adrian Kovacs, Cristina Marullo, Alberto Di Minin and Bart Van Looy I went to check.

We assembled 4,407 articles published between 1985 and mid-2022 that use at least one of the labels, filtered so that fewer than one in ten is off-topic. The field has grown fast: papers on exceptional innovation multiplied 13-fold between 2000 and 2021, against 3.5-fold for business, management and economics as a whole. By 2020, radical and disruptive each had roughly five times the output of the other two labels.

Two bibliometric methods then ask whether the labels mark out different intellectual territory. Co-citation analysis shows what a literature is built on. Bibliographic coupling shows what it is currently about. On both, the labels fail to separate. Different labels rest on the same foundations, and papers on the same topics use different labels, with no reliable correspondence between the word and the subject.

We then read 60 of the most cited papers closely to see whether the definitions do the work the labels do not. A fifth give no explicit definition at all. Among those that do, definitions vary within a label and repeat across labels — the same words, differently branded. So the warning did not work, and if anything the problem has grown with the literature.

The more useful part is the diagnosis. Where definitions are offered, they rest on two underlying dimensions, novelty and impact, and the trouble is that most work treats these as two sides of one coin. If novelty implies impact and impact implies novelty, then the four labels really are one concept and the inconsistency is merely untidy. But they do not imply each other. Off-diagonal cases — genuinely novel inventions that never land, and enormously impactful ones that recombine familiar principles — are common, and collapsing the two dimensions writes them out of the analysis.

Our proposal is to look at trajectories rather than isolated inventions, which separates the dimensions naturally. A novel invention typically starts a line of follow-on work without itself doing much, and the large impact often arrives from a modest change much later. Trajectories can then be characterised by the kind of novelty they introduce: radical novelty establishes new technological principles, while architectural novelty reconfigures existing ones into a new proposition. That distinction has consequences. Radical trajectories move slowly, carry heavy technological uncertainty, and offer weak private incentives. Architectural ones diffuse quickly, resolve fewer unknowns, and reward private R&D well.

Within that frame the four words can coexist without competing. Radical describes a type of novelty. Breakthrough describes exceptional impact at the level of the technology. Discontinuous describes what a trajectory does to the knowledge and resources of incumbents. Disruptive describes the case where it also takes their market share.

Whether anyone adopts a taxonomy is another matter, and we hold no illusions there. The finding that stands regardless is that the labels currently carry no information, so a reader who infers a paper’s content from its chosen word is guessing.

The paper is Radical, Disruptive, Discontinuous, and Breakthrough Innovation: More of the Same?, with Adrian Kovacs, Cristina Marullo, Alberto Di Minin and Bart Van Looy, in Industrial and Corporate Change.
