Dirty air makes fewer inventions, and that raises its price by three quarters

2 September 2026

The economic cost of air pollution is usually measured two ways, and both of them miss something. Mortality and hospitalisation statistics capture the most severe health damage. Estimates of the effect on same-year GDP capture the subtler productivity losses, and those are not small: Dechezlepr&ecirc;tre and co-authors find that a 10% improvement in air quality raises GDP in the same year by 0.8%.

Neither approach can see innovation. The returns to an invention arrive over years or decades, so they never show up in the GDP of the year the polluted air was breathed. If dirty air also depresses invention, the standard bill is missing a line, and the missing line compounds, because innovation is what drives growth in the first place.

Felix Bracht and I try to put a number on it. We build a yearly panel of 977 European NUTS-3 regions — roughly the European equivalent of US counties — from 2001 to 2012, covering about 1.4 million patent families, and measure each region’s exposure to fine particulate matter.

The identification problem is obvious. Polluted regions differ from clean ones in nearly every way that also matters for invention. So we use two weather phenomena instead. Thermal inversions trap pollutants near the ground by reversing the usual temperature-height gradient. Ventilation capability — wind speed times the height of the planetary boundary layer — disperses them. Both are set by atmospheric conditions with no plausible direct route to a region’s patent output, and we control for wind, rain, temperature, humidity and pressure to keep the weather itself out of the estimate.

A fall in fine particulate matter of 0.17 micrograms per cubic metre, which is exactly the average yearly improvement Europe actually achieved over this period, raises patenting by 1.2%. For the average region that is 1.51 more inventions a year. Had air quality simply stayed at its 2001 level, Europe would have produced 1.94% fewer patents over the following decade.

An effect that size invites a deflating reading: perhaps the inventors just move. We can rule that out. Tracking inventor addresses across successive filings, we find no statistically or economically meaningful effect of pollution on inventors moving in or out. The inventions are not relocating. They are not happening.

Two other deflating readings also fail. Firms might cut R&D budgets in anticipation of tighter regulation, which would already sit inside the GDP estimates — but R&D budgets are long-horizon decisions that do not track year-to-year weather, and if that were the mechanism we would expect the low-quality projects to be cut first, which is not what we see. Higher mortality might mechanically reduce inventive capacity, but mortality barely moves in our setting.

Pricing the lost inventions means knowing what an invention is worth over its life, and here the literature is not much help, because private-value estimates disagree with each other badly. Under our preferred assumptions the yearly cost of our reference increase in pollution comes to 0.11% of GDP, about 0.78 times the same-year GDP cost. Counting innovation raises the bill by roughly three quarters.

That figure inherits all the uncertainty of the value estimates underneath it, and it is probably conservative, since patents miss whatever firms choose to keep secret. The honest gap is that we cannot say which channel the damage runs through — fewer hours, worse thinking, or something else — and the right abatement policy depends on that answer.

The paper is Air Pollution and Innovation, with Felix Bracht, in the Journal of Environmental Economics and Management.
